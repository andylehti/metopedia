<p><b>Negative bases and exponentiation</b> are a central issue in the Canonical Order of Operations. The problem is not that negative values cannot be raised to powers. The problem is that linear notation often fails to state whether the negative sign is part of the base or an external sign operation.</p>
<h2 id="base-identity">Base identity</h2>
<p>In exponentiation, the base is the object acted upon by the exponent. Canonical notation requires that this object be identified before the exponent is applied.</p>

<h2 id="why-the-distinction-matters">Why the distinction matters</h2>
<p>The expressions <math>-x^2&lt;/math> and &lt;math>(-x)^2&lt;/math> can produce different results. Standard notation already recognizes this distinction, but COO treats it as part of a wider rule: the sign must not move into or out of the base by implication.</p>
<h2 id="fractional-exponents">Fractional exponents</h2>
<p>The distinction becomes more important when fractional exponents are used. A fractional exponent represents a root, and roots of negative quantities can trigger a move from real-number arithmetic to complex-number interpretation. COO therefore requires explicit notation before such a move is made.</p>
<div class="wikitable-wrap"><table class="wikitable"><tr><th>Expression type</th><th>Canonical concern</th></tr><tr><td><math>x^{1/2}&lt;/math></td><td>The base is positive, zero, symbolic, or otherwise constrained by context.</td></tr><tr><td><math>-x^{1/2}&lt;/math></td><td>The sign is external to the root unless grouping says otherwise.</td></tr><tr><td><math>(-x)^{1/2}&lt;/math></td><td>The grouped signed quantity is under the fractional exponent.</td></tr></table></div>
<h2 id="relation-to-imaginary-numbers">Relation to imaginary numbers</h2>
<p>The Canonical Order manuscript argues that some uses of imaginary numbers are introduced prematurely because sign scope and root notation are not made explicit before evaluation. This does not deny the usefulness of complex numbers in advanced mathematics. It argues that basic arithmetic should first exhaust clear real-number structure before moving into complex-number interpretation.</p>
