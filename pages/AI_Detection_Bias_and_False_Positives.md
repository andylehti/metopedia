<p><b><i>AI-Detection Bias and False Positives: Comparing 2016 Human, 2026 AI, and 2007 Student Essays Across Common Detectors</i></b> is a 2026 paper by Andrew Lehti examining the reliability of common AI-authorship detectors when applied to human-written and AI-generated text samples.<sup id="cite_ref-lehti-doi_1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-lehti-doi">[1]</a></sup> The study compares detector outputs on older human writing, contemporary AI writing, informal student writing, and additional control texts. It argues that many detectors appear to penalize formal structure, grammatical consistency, and polished academic style rather than identifying a stable signature of machine authorship.</p>
<p>The paper is situated within broader debate over AI-content detection in education. Published research has found that available AI-detection tools can be unreliable, inconsistent, and vulnerable to paraphrasing or obfuscation.<sup id="cite_ref-weber_1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-weber">[2]</a></sup> Other research has reported bias against non-native English writing, raising concerns about fairness in academic and evaluative settings.<sup id="cite_ref-liang_1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-liang">[3]</a></sup></p>
<p>The central finding of Lehti's paper is that a polished human essay from 2016 received a higher average AI-detection score than a 2026 AI-generated essay. The paper interprets this as evidence of a “polish penalty”: the tendency of detectors to associate structural competence, formal tone, and regular formatting with artificial generation.<sup id="cite_ref-lehti-doi_2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-lehti-doi">[1]</a></sup></p>
<h2 id="background">Background</h2>
<p>AI-detection systems are software tools that attempt to estimate whether text was written by a human, generated by a language model, or produced through a mixture of human and machine assistance. These tools are often used in academic settings to support plagiarism screening, authorship review, and academic-integrity investigations. Their outputs are commonly expressed as percentages, risk labels, or probability bands.</p>
<p>The growth of generative language models increased pressure on schools, universities, publishers, and online platforms to distinguish human writing from model-generated writing. In practice, this task is difficult because modern language models are trained on large corpora of human writing, including academic prose, web articles, essays, forum posts, documentation, and informal discussion. A model's output can resemble the average style of the same written ecosystem from which human writers also learn.</p>
<p>Lehti's paper argues that this creates a convergence problem. AI systems learn from human writing; human writers increasingly read AI-influenced text; and ordinary writing tools now include grammar correction, tone rewriting, autocomplete, and one-click revision. As a result, the boundary between “human style” and “machine style” becomes less stable over time.<sup id="cite_ref-lehti-doi_3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-lehti-doi">[1]</a></sup></p>
<h2 id="publication-and-context">Publication and context</h2>
<p>The paper is part of Lehti's broader Metopedia and cognitive-psychology corpus. It includes an introductory advisory on “Cognitive Impasse,” a concept used by the author to describe resistance to ideas that contradict prior beliefs. The paper also describes the author's method as “Extrapolative Trial by Error,” a process in which independent observation and synthesis precede review of external academic literature.<sup id="cite_ref-lehti-doi_4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-lehti-doi">[1]</a></sup></p>
<p>Although those framing sections are unusual for a conventional detector-evaluation paper, the empirical core of the document is a comparative table of detector outputs across multiple writing samples. The article also includes appendices containing control cases, full detector tables, error-rate summaries, and graphs.</p>
<h2 id="research-question">Research question</h2>
<p>The study asks whether public and commercial AI-detection systems can reliably distinguish AI-generated content from older and contemporary human-generated writing. It focuses on false positives, detector disagreement, and the possibility that detectors may classify polished human writing as AI-generated because of surface-level features.</p>
<p>The specific concerns examined include:</p>
<ul>
<li>whether detector outputs agree across tools;</li>
<li>whether older pre-ChatGPT human writing is misclassified as AI;</li>
<li>whether informal or lower-structure writing is more likely to be treated as human;</li>
<li>whether formatting, punctuation, and academic polish affect AI scores;</li>
<li>whether “humanizer” tools reduce or increase AI-detection scores;</li>
<li>whether academic institutions should use AI detectors as determinative evidence.</li>
</ul>

<h2 id="stylistic-ecosystem-convergence">Stylistic ecosystem convergence</h2>
<p>One of the paper's main interpretive sections argues that AI detection is structurally unstable because both humans and AI participate in the same writing ecosystem. Language models are trained on human writing from online platforms, academic works, social media, forums, and professional writing. Human writers, in turn, learn from the same public internet and increasingly encounter AI-generated prose.</p>
<p>The paper describes this as a feedback loop:</p>
<ol>
<li>AI produces structured text.</li>
<li>Humans read and absorb the structures.</li>
<li>Humans adopt some of those structures.</li>
<li>Human writing moves closer to AI training distributions.</li>
<li>Detectors must distinguish overlapping distributions.</li>
</ol>
<p>This argument resembles broader concerns in machine-learning classification: when two classes become statistically entangled, classifiers lose stable decision boundaries. In authorship detection, the relevant signal is not only what a text looks like, but whether its stylistic features uniquely identify its source. Lehti argues that they increasingly do not.<sup id="cite_ref-lehti-doi_14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-lehti-doi">[1]</a></sup></p>
<h2 id="formatting-and-structural-bias">Formatting and structural bias</h2>
<p>The paper argues that formatting choices can affect detector scores even when semantic content remains unchanged. Reported factors include:</p>
<ul>
<li>greater length;</li>
<li>em-dash frequency;</li>
<li>semicolon and colon usage;</li>
<li>structured section headings;</li>
<li>markdown formatting;</li>
<li>vocabulary density;</li>
<li>lexical precision;</li>
<li>consistent punctuation;</li>
<li>grammatical confidence.</li>
</ul>
<p>Lehti describes this as a practical institutional concern because a student or writer might be penalized for writing clearly, using formal organization, or presenting work in a clean academic format.<sup id="cite_ref-lehti-doi_15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-lehti-doi">[1]</a></sup></p>
<h2 id="ai-authorship-and-ai-assisted-revision">AI authorship and AI-assisted revision</h2>
<p>The paper distinguishes full AI authorship from AI-assisted revision. Full AI authorship is described as a model generating the main substance of a work from limited prompting. AI-assisted revision is described as human authorship followed by software-based improvements to clarity, grammar, flow, tone, punctuation, or redundancy.</p>
<p>This distinction is significant because modern writing environments often include AI-like correction systems. Email clients, word processors, grammar checkers, and online editors may suggest rewritten sentences, tone adjustments, and punctuation changes. These changes can regularize a document without replacing the author's ideas.</p>
<p>Lehti argues that detection systems often treat authorship and revision as the same category. A human-authored document that has been lightly smoothed may acquire features that detectors associate with generated text: regular syntax, coherent structure, reduced redundancy, and consistent punctuation.<sup id="cite_ref-lehti-doi_16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-lehti-doi">[1]</a></sup></p>
<h2 id="the-polish-penalty-hypothesis">The polish penalty hypothesis</h2>
<p>The “polish penalty” is the paper's name for the observed tendency of AI detectors to assign higher AI probabilities to cleaner, more structured writing. Under this hypothesis, detectors may interpret formal competence as artificiality.</p>
<p>The paper contrasts three kinds of samples:</p>
<div class="wikitable-wrap"><table class="wikitable"><tr><th>Writing type</th><th>Detector tendency in the study</th><th>Interpretation</th></tr><tr><td>Informal, irregular, error-prone writing</td><td>Usually scored as human</td><td>Irregularity is treated as a human signal</td></tr><tr><td>Semi-formal human writing</td><td>Often scored as partially or highly AI</td><td>Structure and polish raise suspicion</td></tr><tr><td>AI-generated semi-formal writing</td><td>Scored inconsistently, sometimes lower than human writing</td><td>Detectors do not share a stable AI signal</td></tr></table></div>
<p>The hypothesis does not require that every detector behave identically. Instead, it claims that a general relationship appears across the study: as structural competence increases, AI attribution becomes more likely.<sup id="cite_ref-lehti-doi_17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-lehti-doi">[1]</a></sup></p>
<h2 id="humanization-paradox">Humanization paradox</h2>
<p>The paper reports an additional test in which a human-written academic text initially received a mean AI score of 44.7%. After being processed through AI “humanization” or detector-bypass tools, the mean AI score increased to 76.4%.</p>
<p>Lehti describes this as paradoxical because a tool intended to make text appear more human made the human text appear more AI-generated. The paper interprets this as evidence that detectors are highly sensitive to surface-level statistical changes and may not be measuring authorship origin directly.<sup id="cite_ref-lehti-doi_18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-lehti-doi">[1]</a></sup></p>
<h2 id="academic-implications">Academic implications</h2>
<p>The paper argues that AI detectors should not be used as determinative evidence in academic misconduct cases. The reasoning is based on three claims:</p>
<ol>
<li>false positives can accuse human writers of misconduct;</li>
<li>detector disagreement makes tool choice outcome-determinative;</li>
<li>polished academic writing can be misread as AI-generated writing.</li>
</ol>
<p>This position is consistent with caution expressed by outside institutions and researchers. Vanderbilt University disabled Turnitin's AI detector in 2023, citing concerns about transparency, false positives, and the consequences of a 1% false-positive rate when applied to large numbers of student papers.<sup id="cite_ref-vanderbilt_1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-vanderbilt">[4]</a></sup> Turnitin has published its own explanation of false positives and its AI-writing report behavior, including thresholds designed to reduce false-positive risk.<sup id="cite_ref-turnitin-fp_1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-turnitin-fp">[5]</a></sup><sup id="cite_ref-turnitin-report_1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-turnitin-report">[6]</a></sup></p>
<h2 id="relation-to-existing-research">Relation to existing research</h2>
<p>Lehti's findings align with several external concerns in the literature:</p>
<ul>
<li>Weber-Wulff et al. tested multiple AI-generated-text detectors and concluded that available tools were not accurate or reliable enough for dependable academic use.<sup id="cite_ref-weber_2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-weber">[2]</a></sup></li>
<li>Liang et al. found that GPT detectors misclassified non-native English writing as AI-generated and warned against unfair evaluative use.<sup id="cite_ref-liang_2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-liang">[3]</a></sup></li>
<li>Institutional guidance has often urged instructors to treat detector scores as signals rather than proof.<sup id="cite_ref-vanderbilt_2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-vanderbilt">[4]</a></sup></li>
<li>Detector companies themselves acknowledge the possibility of false positives, although they may claim lower rates than independent or field-level critiques suggest.<sup id="cite_ref-turnitin-fp_2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-turnitin-fp">[5]</a></sup></li>
</ul>
<p>Lehti's contribution is narrower and more autobiographical in sample selection, but it adds a specific claim: polished pre-AI human writing can be scored as AI at a higher mean level than an AI-generated essay tested in the same comparison.<sup id="cite_ref-lehti-doi_19" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-lehti-doi">[1]</a></sup></p>
<h2 id="limitations">Limitations</h2>
<p>The paper has several limitations:</p>
<ul>
<li><b>Small sample set.</b> The core study uses a limited number of texts, including personally selected examples.</li>
<li><b>Detector opacity.</b> The internal criteria of commercial detectors are not independently known.</li>
<li><b>Changing tools.</b> AI detectors update over time, so exact scores may not reproduce later.</li>
<li><b>Input limits.</b> Some tools required chunking or limited submissions, which may affect comparability.</li>
<li><b>No blind institutional sample.</b> The study does not use a large randomized corpus of verified human and AI writing.</li>
<li><b>Percentage comparability.</b> Different tools may define “AI percentage” differently.</li>
<li><b>Model-based judgments.</b> ChatGPT and Gemini outputs are not detector scores in the same sense as commercial detector percentages.</li>
</ul>
<p>These limitations do not eliminate the paper's concern about false positives, but they affect how broadly its numeric results can be generalized.</p>
<h2 id="terminology">Terminology</h2>
<p>; AI detector : A tool that estimates whether text was generated by artificial intelligence.</p>
<p>; False positive : A human-written text incorrectly classified as AI-generated.</p>
<p>; False negative : AI-generated text incorrectly classified as human-written.</p>
<p>; Polish penalty : Lehti's proposed term for the tendency of detectors to assign higher AI probability to cleaner, more structured, or more academically polished writing.</p>
<p>; Stylistic convergence : The overlap between human and AI writing styles caused by shared corpora, AI-assisted writing tools, and human exposure to AI-generated prose.</p>
<p>; AI-assisted revision : Human-authored writing that has been edited, clarified, or polished with machine assistance, without the machine generating the core argument or evidence.</p>
