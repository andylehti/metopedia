<div class="hatnote"><i>Main article:</i> <a href="/Canonical_Order_of_Operations/">Canonical Order of Operations</a></div>
<p>The <b>Law of Implicit Unity</b> is a rule in the <a href="/Canonical_Order_of_Operations/">Canonical Order of Operations</a> stating that a number, variable, or grouped term may be treated as having an implicit exponent of one. The rule is expressed as:</p>
<div class="tex-math math-display-source" data-display="block" data-tex="x=x^1">x=x^1</div>
<p>The law does not change the value of a term. Its purpose is interpretive: it makes the base of an exponent visible before exponent rules are applied.</p>
<h2 id="role-in-coo">Role in COO</h2>
<p>The Canonical Order uses the law to prevent exponentiation from being applied to an unclear or shifting base. In this system, the first task is to identify the unit being acted upon; the second task is to apply the exponent law.</p>

<h2 id="negative-signs">Negative signs</h2>
<p>The law is used with special care when a negative sign appears near an exponent. In ordinary notation, <span class="tex-math math-inline-source" data-display="inline" data-tex="-x^2">-x^2</span> and <span class="tex-math math-inline-source" data-display="inline" data-tex="(-x)^2">(-x)^2</span> are different expressions. COO treats this as a general principle rather than a local exception: the written form must decide whether the base is <span class="tex-math math-inline-source" data-display="inline" data-tex="x">x</span>, <span class="tex-math math-inline-source" data-display="inline" data-tex="-x">-x</span>, or a larger grouped object.</p>

<h2 id="rationale">Rationale</h2>
<p>The rule is meant to make exponentiation reversible and inspectable. By treating every base as carrying an implicit first power, COO can apply index laws without silently changing the object being acted upon.</p>
