<div class="hatnote"><i>Main article:</i> <a href="/Canonical_Order_of_Operations/">Canonical Order of Operations</a></div>
<p><b>Misconceptions on mathematical operations</b> are recurring interpretive errors that arise when arithmetic notation is taught as a sequence of mechanical rules rather than as a grammar of operations. In the Canonical Order framework, these misconceptions are treated as evidence that the Standard Order is often too compressed to explain what an expression is doing.</p>
<h2 id="common-misconceptions">Common misconceptions</h2>
<div class="wikitable-wrap"><table class="wikitable"><tr><th>Misconception</th><th>Canonical correction</th></tr><tr><td>Exponentiation is repeated multiplication.</td><td>Repeated multiplication is a useful model for positive integer exponents, but exponentiation is a broader operation that also includes zero, negative, and fractional exponents.</td></tr><tr><td>A minus sign is always part of the number beside it.</td><td>A negative sign can be a sign operator or part of a grouped signed quantity. The notation must show which role it has.</td></tr><tr><td>Parentheses merely mean “do this first.”</td><td>Parentheses also define the object that later operations act upon.</td></tr><tr><td>Roots are separate from powers.</td><td>Roots can be written as fractional powers: <span class="tex-math math-inline-source" data-display="inline" data-tex="\sqrt[n]{x}=x^{\dfrac{1}{n}}">\sqrt[n]{x}=x^{\dfrac{1}{n}}</span>.</td></tr><tr><td>The radical symbol is necessary.</td><td>COO treats the radical symbol as redundant because fractional exponents express the same operation more systematically.</td></tr><tr><td>Calculator output defines mathematical grammar.</td><td>Calculators follow input syntax and precedence rules; they do not settle whether the notation was well formed.</td></tr><tr><td>Ambiguous expressions have one natural answer.</td><td>Ambiguous expressions require either a convention or a rewrite that makes structure explicit.</td></tr></table></div>
<h2 id="multiplication-and-exponentiation">Multiplication and exponentiation</h2>
<p>A common teaching shortcut defines exponentiation as repeated multiplication. This is helpful for expressions such as <span class="tex-math math-inline-source" data-display="inline" data-tex="2^3=2\times2\times2">2^3=2\times2\times2</span>, but it does not explain <span class="tex-math math-inline-source" data-display="inline" data-tex="2^0">2^0</span>, <span class="tex-math math-inline-source" data-display="inline" data-tex="2^{-3}">2^{-3}</span>, or <span class="tex-math math-inline-source" data-display="inline" data-tex="2^{\dfrac{1}{2}}">2^{\dfrac{1}{2}}</span>. COO treats this shortcut as one source of later confusion.</p>
<h2 id="signs-and-bases">Signs and bases</h2>
<p>The expression <span class="tex-math math-inline-source" data-display="inline" data-tex="-x^2">-x^2</span> is not the same as <span class="tex-math math-inline-source" data-display="inline" data-tex="(-x)^2">(-x)^2</span>. The difference is not a special trick; it is a difference in base identity. In the first expression the base is <span class="tex-math math-inline-source" data-display="inline" data-tex="x">x</span>. In the second expression the base is the grouped signed term <span class="tex-math math-inline-source" data-display="inline" data-tex="-x">-x</span>.</p>
<h2 id="roots-and-fractional-powers">Roots and fractional powers</h2>
<p>Root notation often conceals its connection to exponentiation. The canonical rewrite makes that connection immediate:</p>
<div class="tex-math math-display-source" data-display="block" data-tex="\sqrt[5]{x^2}=x^{\dfrac{2}{5}}">\sqrt[5]{x^2}=x^{\dfrac{2}{5}}</div>
<p>Once roots are written as powers, the ordinary laws of indices can be applied without switching notation systems.</p>
<h2 id="parentheses-and-grouping">Parentheses and grouping</h2>
<p>Parentheses should not be treated as a ritual instruction to perform whatever is inside first. They are also a structural marker. They define a unit. In exponentiation, identifying that unit is essential because the exponent acts only on its base.</p>
