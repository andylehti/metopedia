**Censorship of Academic Papers on Figshare and Zenodo** is a Metopedia investigative evidence page concerning the removal, disappearance, or practical inaccessibility of DOI-linked research records on Figshare and Zenodo after publication of works critical of NASA's Apollo Moon-landing record. The page examines whether repository actions were ordinary moderation, spam enforcement, research-integrity enforcement, repository-scope enforcement, or a form of viewpoint-sensitive academic censorship.

The inquiry is not whether every Apollo-critical claim is correct. The inquiry is whether open-research repositories that advertise preservation, DOI persistence, and accountability should remove controversial research with vague labels such as "spam," "inappropriate content," or "terms violation" without providing an itemized explanation for each affected work.

The working thesis is narrow: **controversial research may be wrong, weak, speculative, or ultimately disproven, but it is not misinformation merely because it challenges an official or dominant narrative.** Scientific integrity requires that opposing claims be inspectable, criticized, corrected, replicated, or rejected on stated grounds. When repository infrastructure removes the record without preserving meaningful metadata, the public cannot examine the claim or the reason for its removal.

## Investigative thesis

Repository-level integrity investigation.

The comparison is between two kinds of platform behavior:

| Platform type | Observed pattern in the supplied search record | Investigative relevance |
|---|---|---|
| Research-sharing platforms | ResearchGate and Academia.edu surfaced Apollo-critical or Apollo-denial material quickly, including material for and against the subject. | These platforms provide a comparison case showing that controversial Apollo-critical literature can remain visible, searchable, and contestable. |
| DOI-focused open repositories | Zenodo and Figshare produced sparse, unstable, removed, or non-qualifying results after extended AI-assisted and human searches. | These platforms claim stronger preservation and DOI accountability; therefore opaque removal or disappearance is more significant. |

The strongest evidence is not a single removal. The evidence is the pattern:

1. ResearchGate and Academia.edu produced retrievable Apollo-critical results within minutes during public web searches.
2. AI-assisted deep research and live-searching of Zenodo and Figshare reportedly continued for approximately seven hours before returning no substantial historical corpus of DOI-hosted Apollo-critical work on those two repositories.
3. Human follow-up searches likewise failed to locate a meaningful body of Moon-landing-critical material on Zenodo and Figshare.
4. Figshare records connected to Lehti's Apollo and photo-forensic work were reported as removed, unavailable, or displaying Figshare removal notices.
5. Zenodo showed removed records classified as spam, while the only later accessible Apollo-critical Zenodo cluster found in the supplied investigation was from April 6, 2026, by N-K Sciences.
6. The repositories' stated goals of open research, persistence, and accountability conflict with vague or non-itemized removals.

The record does not prove that NASA directed the removals. It does support a formal question: **Are open-research repositories preserving controversial criticism as part of the scientific record, or are they using broad policy discretion to erase material that should remain inspectable?**

## Executive summary

The evidence record supports five findings. A later evidence memo supplied for this page restates the main structure: Figshare is the clearest removal evidence, Zenodo is a weaker but relevant scarcity-and-tombstone concern, and the central question is whether controversial criticism remains available for examination and rebuttal.[^evidence-extracted-points]

First, Figshare hosted DOI-linked works by Andrew Lehti concerning Apollo photographs, lunar image forensics, Image Degradation Analysis, and related institutional critiques. Several search reports identify Figshare DOI records connected to this material as removed, unavailable, or displaying terms-violation notices.[^evidence-find-nasa][^evidence-additional]

Second, Zenodo searches repeatedly surfaced ordinary lunar-science material, mission-planning material, and misinformation-discussion material, but did not produce a stable historical corpus of DOI-linked Apollo-critical works. One Zenodo record mentioning the Moon-landing hoax treated it as an example of misinformation rather than as a supported thesis.[^evidence-find-nasa][^evidence-four-platform]

Third, later searches found an April 6, 2026 Zenodo cluster by N-K Sciences, including records alleging NASA fake Moon soil samples and Apollo/Artemis fraud. These records are relevant as accessible Apollo-critical exceptions, but their late date makes them weak evidence for a long-standing Zenodo archive of such criticism.[^evidence-additional]

Fourth, ResearchGate and Academia.edu acted as comparison platforms. A conservative search report found multiple Academia.edu items and ResearchGate items explicitly arguing that Apollo was faked, staged, composited, or otherwise not what the public was told. None of those included items were removed or behind login at the time of inspection, although one ResearchGate item carried a retraction notice.[^evidence-rg-academia]

Fifth, the platform responses and tombstone notices did not provide the itemized disclosure needed to evaluate whether removals were based on spam, duplication, copyright, plagiarism, research integrity, legal risk, defamation, privacy, repository scope, or viewpoint-sensitive enforcement.

### Reading the pattern

The Figshare set is the strongest removal evidence. Multiple DOI-linked records tied to Apollo imagery, lunar-image forensics, and NASA narrative criticism were identified, and later reports state that those pages displayed removal or access-disabled notices under Figshare’s terms and conditions. The ResearchGate and Academia.edu materials provide the comparison set: similar Apollo-critical or Apollo-skeptical material could be located and retrieved there, including several live ResearchGate items, one retracted ResearchGate item, and multiple Academia.edu items.

Zenodo is different. The two live Zenodo examples were found only in the deeper follow-up search and both came from the same recent N-K Sciences cluster. They are relevant because they show that some NASA-critical Apollo material was present on Zenodo, but they do not establish a broad historical archive of Apollo criticism on that platform. The evidence still indicates that accessible Zenodo criticism was sparse compared with conventional lunar research and compared with the faster retrieval of related material on ResearchGate and Academia.edu.

## Scope and claim standard

This table distinguishes four levels of claim.

| Claim level | Meaning | Status in this investigation |
|---|---|---|
| Documented fact | A platform notice, DOI, correspondence, or search report exists. | Supported where copied, cited, or listed. |
| Search finding | A report states that searches found or did not find certain records. | Supported as a search finding, not as an absolute proof of absence. |
| Pattern inference | The evidence suggests selective accessibility or unstable preservation. | Supported as an inference requiring further verification. |
| Censorship conclusion | A platform removed work because of its viewpoint or institutional target. | Plausible hypothesis, not established fact without internal records. |

This distinction is essential. The investigation is strongest when it separates evidence from interpretation.

## Why opposing views are part of science

Science is not a loyalty system. It is a method for testing claims against evidence, logic, measurement, replication, and criticism. A claim does not become misinformation merely because it opposes the current narrative. It becomes misinformation when it is knowingly false, deceptively framed, unsupported while presented as established fact, or insulated from correction.

Opposing views have several legitimate roles in science:

* they test the strength of accepted explanations;
* they reveal missing data, weak assumptions, and unexamined conventions;
* they force evidence to be shown rather than merely invoked;
* they expose where institutions rely on authority rather than reproducibility;
* they allow error correction when consensus has hardened into doctrine.

A repository does not have to endorse a paper in order to preserve it. Preservation means the claim can be inspected. If the work is weak, the answer is criticism, replication failure, retraction, expression of concern, or a clear research-integrity note. If the work violates policy, the answer is an itemized explanation. Removal without meaningful metadata creates a different problem: the work cannot be examined, and the removal cannot be audited.

In this investigation, the question is therefore not whether Apollo criticism is true. The question is whether academic infrastructure allows controversial criticism to remain part of the inspectable record.

## Background: when open research disappears

Open research repositories are not ordinary file hosts. They present themselves as part of the scholarly record: places where research can be cited, preserved, inspected, challenged, and revisited. When a repository assigns a DOI, the work becomes more than an upload. It becomes a traceable academic object. When these connections disappear, so do the peer-review process, accessibility, and credibility.

Zenodo describes itself as an open research repository built and operated by CERN and OpenAIRE, with uploads assigned Digital Object Identifiers and stored in CERN's Data Centre.[^zenodo-home] Zenodo's public materials state that it was launched through the OpenAIRE project, with CERN providing repository capability.[^zenodo-about]

Figshare describes itself as repository infrastructure for sharing, showcasing, and managing research outputs.[^figshare-plus] Digital Science materials state that Figshare is part of Digital Science and that Figshare provides repository infrastructure for institutions.[^digital-science-state-open-data] Figshare also publishes retention and backup representations, including that public items are retained for the lifetime of the repository, that metadata can remain accessible when data are no longer available, and that backups are performed nightly to Figshare-managed AWS S3 buckets with versioning for restoration.[^figshare-retention][^figshare-fair][^figshare-backups]

These claims create a public expectation: if research is removed, the removal should be specific, traceable, and reviewable. A DOI-linked work should not vanish behind a generic notice. Readers should be able to know what existed, who created it, what identifier it carried, when it was removed, and why.

The issue is not whether repositories may moderate. They can. The issue is whether repositories that market permanence, openness, and scholarly accountability can remove academic work without giving the author or public a meaningful explanation. When the removed work concerns a controversial institutional subject, that gap becomes an issue of integrity.

## Policy framework and the problem of broad removal clauses

Zenodo's Terms of Use include a broad clause stating that CERN may remove or block content and restrict user access at its discretion when it deems content inappropriate, insufficiently protected, interfering with operations, violating terms, or violating applicable law.[^zenodo-terms] Zenodo's policy page states that withdrawal is exceptional, that the reason for withdrawal should be indicated on a tombstone page, and that the DOI and original URL are retained.[^zenodo-policies] Zenodo's help page lists removal reasons including spam, withdrawal/deaccession, takedown requests, copyright infringement, plagiarism, scientific misconduct, violation of terms, personal data, transfer, duplicate submissions, and legal or regulatory compliance.[^zenodo-removal-reasons]

Figshare's Terms and Conditions include a "Content You Must Not Submit" section covering many categories, including rights violations, defamation, privacy violations, illegal content, malware, abusive or harmful content, sensitive personal information, false or misleading content, harm to Figshare or affiliates, AI-generated or duplicate publication content, and violation of acceptable-use policies.[^figshare-terms]

The breadth of these clauses makes itemized explanation essential. A generic reference to a broad policy does not identify the work, the clause, the alleged violation, or the evidence. It does not distinguish plagiarism from spam, copyright risk from defamation risk, duplicate upload from scientific-misconduct concern, or ordinary moderation from viewpoint-sensitive suppression.

Without that specificity, the public cannot inspect the decision. The author cannot correct the alleged problem. Future researchers cannot tell whether the removed work was flawed, unlawful, duplicative, controversial, inconvenient, or simply unwanted.

## Figshare's account-disabled response

Figshare's response to Andrew Lehti illustrates the central transparency issue. The response refers to broad policy categories but does not identify which item violated which clause, what content was considered inappropriate, whether the concern involved copyright, duplication, research integrity, authorship, factuality, platform abuse, or any external complaint.

<div class="quote-box" markdown="1">

**Figshare response to Andrew Lehti**

Hello Andrew,

Hello,

We have checked, and your account has been disabled for violating Figshare's Terms and Conditions (https://figshare.com/terms) — please refer in particular to the "Acceptable Use" and "Content You Must Not Submit" sections, which are most likely to be of relevance — for posting inappropriate content (https://help.figshare.com/article/inappropriate-content-on-figshare).

Below, you can also find details of the key principles of our acceptable publication guidelines:

**Academic research:** Figshare should be used for publishing the outputs of academic research that benefit the research community.

**Responsible research practices, research integrity and publication ethics:** Figshare should be used for publishing research that is consistent with the principles of responsible research, research integrity and publication ethics, and not work that might undermine such principles.

**Non-commercial:** Figshare should be used solely for personal, non-commercial scholarly purposes.

**Authorship:** Figshare should be used to publish work that has been authored by the submitter.

**Originality:** Figshare should be used to publish the original work of the author not, for example, work that has been copied (plagiarism, self-plagiarism or text recycling) or has already been published already, nor for making repetitive uploads of substantially the same material.

We are unable to provide further information at this time.

</div>

This response is important because it gives a policy area but not an accountable decision record. It does not provide an itemized list of affected works. It does not explain whether all removed works violated the same rule. It does not identify a complaint, duplicate-content issue, authorship dispute, copyright concern, research-integrity finding, or legal basis. It also closes with the statement that Figshare is "unable to provide further information at this time."

For an ordinary website, that might be treated as routine account moderation. For a DOI-linked research repository, it is more serious. The removal affects citation records, metadata trails, scholarly access, and the ability of readers to inspect controversial claims. A repository that preserves research must also preserve the accountability record when it removes research.

## Search audit: what was found and not found

### Search method and asymmetry

The investigation used AI-assisted deep research, live web search, platform searches, direct DOI checks, and human follow-up. Lehti reports that ResearchGate and Academia.edu results were found within minutes, while AI-assisted searches through Zenodo and Figshare continued for approximately seven hours before returning no substantial historical corpus of accessible Apollo-critical DOI records. Human follow-up likewise failed to locate a comparable body of criticism on Zenodo or Figshare.

This asymmetry does not prove censorship by itself. It does create an investigative lead because the repositories with stronger preservation claims appeared to have the weaker visible record for controversial criticism.

### Search terms and inclusion rules

Searches included platform-domain queries and denial-specific terms such as:

* `moon landing hoax`
* `NASA hoax`
* `fake moon landing`
* `staged moon landing`
* `We Never Went to the Moon`
* `Bart Sibrel`
* `Bill Kaysing`
* `alunizaje falso`
* `Apollo staged`
* `artificial lighting Apollo`
* exact DOI searches
* exact title searches

For Zenodo and Figshare, the strongest search standard required a platform-hosted DOI record. For ResearchGate and Academia.edu, the search reports accepted platform-hosted items even when no DOI was shown, because those platforms do not function primarily as DOI repositories.

## AI-assisted source discovery: Andrew Lehti as a recurring result

One Google Gemini DeepResearch Pro report was repository-focused rather than biographical. It was not framed as a search for Andrew Lehti. Yet the report repeatedly surfaced Lehti's Figshare and ResearchGate work as the main discoverable Apollo-critical research thread in the Zenodo/Figshare search environment.[^evidence-gemini-forensic]

This matters for the investigative narrative because it suggests that Lehti did not appear as a peripheral name added after the fact. In the AI-assisted search record, his work became the dominant identifiable trace of Apollo-critical DOI-linked material on Figshare.

The Gemini report described the result this way:

> A primary figure in the dissident research landscape on Figshare is Andrew Lehti, an independent cognitive researcher and forensic analyst. Lehti's body of work, comprising 39 public contributions, focuses on the psychological and technical frameworks that prevent or allow for the questioning of historical narratives.

The same report identified Lehti's IDA paper as the strongest technical opposition it found in that repository-centered search:

> The most technical and direct opposition to the moon landing narrative found in the research material is Lehti's paper titled "Image Degradation Analysis (IDA): Forensic Applications for Light Physics" (DOI: 10.6084/m9.figshare.31323694).

It characterized the IDA argument as a forensic light-physics claim rather than a purely rhetorical denial claim:

> Unlike standard Error Level Analysis (ELA), which detects digital splicing by identifying inconsistent compression rates, IDA focuses on the physical properties of light as it interacts with objects in a scene.

The report also described the interpretive conflict around dissent:

> The debate over the moon landings is frequently framed by mainstream academia not as a technical or historical inquiry, but as a psychological phenomenon.

And it warned that this framing can convert a technical dispute into a psychological dismissal:

> This academic strategy effectively pathologizes dissent, suggesting that individuals who question the authenticity of the moon landing footage are suffering from "cognitive impasse" or are influenced by "celebrity worship" and "social proof" biases.

These excerpts are not treated here as proof that the IDA claim is correct. Their evidentiary value is narrower: when AI-assisted research searched the repository landscape for Apollo-critical materials, the results repeatedly converged on Lehti's work, while comparable Zenodo and Figshare material by other authors was scarce, removed, inaccessible, or difficult to verify.

## Defensive publication and parallel accountability

The CollectiveOS example illustrates a broader reason independent researchers use Zenodo and similar repositories for defensive publication. A DOI can convert a work into a dated scholarly artifact. In principle, this lets research bypass traditional gatekeepers by making a claim citable, timestamped, inspectable, and historically traceable.

The quoted principle is central to this investigation:

> The "CollectiveOS" initiative's use of Zenodo for "defensive publication" represents a grassroots movement toward this new framework of integrity. By utilizing DOIs to cement research as a "scholarly, historical artifact," independent scientists are creating a parallel system of accountability that bypasses traditional institutional gatekeepers.

That promise fails if DOI-linked works can be removed under vague labels without itemized disclosure. In that case, the DOI preserves only a trace of institutional action, not the research record itself. A tombstone can prove that something existed, but without title, author, metadata, files, reason, and appeal path, it does not preserve the scholarly object needed for future accountability.

### Interpretation of the Zenodo record

The Zenodo record shows a stronger tombstone practice than a dead link because it preserves a removal classification and identifier. The weakness is that "spam" is not self-explanatory. If the removed record was a non-research abuse upload, the label may be ordinary enforcement. If the record was a scholarly or quasi-scholarly work, the label would need explanation.

The conflict is sharpened by Zenodo's stated role as an open repository. A record labeled spam is not merely rejected; it is removed from the inspectable scholarly record. The itemized question is therefore: what exactly made the record spam?

## Censorship question

The censorship claim arises from the gap between open-repository promises and opaque removal practice. A repository can have a legitimate right to remove spam, unlawful material, copyright violations, plagiarism, sensitive personal data, or other prohibited content. The question here is whether the affected works were actually in those categories, and whether the platforms supplied enough information for the author, readers, and DOI users to inspect the decision.

A removal looks more like ordinary moderation when:

* the platform identifies the exact item;
* the platform cites the exact policy clause;
* the platform explains how the work violated the clause;
* the DOI resolves to a meaningful tombstone;
* metadata remains usable;
* the author receives an export or restoration path;
* the appeal process is clear.

A removal looks more like censorship when:

* the content concerns a controversial institutional subject;
* the removal follows the publication of criticism;
* the platform uses a broad label such as "spam" or "inappropriate content" without itemized analysis;
* unrelated works are removed together;
* DOI pages no longer identify the specific works;
* search visibility or metadata persistence is degraded;
* the platform refuses to provide further information.

The present record contains enough evidence to justify investigation into censorship and repository accountability. It does not contain enough evidence, by itself, to identify the internal decision-maker, prove viewpoint intent, or prove external pressure.

## Conflict-of-interest concerns

### Zenodo, CERN, NASA, and the U.S. government

Zenodo is operated by CERN and OpenAIRE.[^zenodo-home][^zenodo-about] CERN and NASA have publicly collaborated on open-science initiatives, including a 2023 CERN-NASA open science summit and a framework agreement concerning open science collaboration.[^cern-nasa-news][^nasa-cern-framework] CERN also has a documented history of U.S. government participation and funding support for CERN-related projects, including U.S. contributions to the Large Hadron Collider program through the Department of Energy and National Science Foundation.[^us-cern-funding][^cern-us-lhc]

The conflict-of-interest question is not whether NASA directly ordered a Zenodo removal. That has not been established. The narrower and more defensible question is whether a CERN-operated repository that collaborates with NASA on open science should provide heightened transparency when removing academic criticism of NASA-related historical claims.

### Figshare, Digital Science, NASA, and U.S. government agencies

Figshare is part of Digital Science, and Digital Science markets research products to institutions, governments, funders, and research organizations.[^digital-science-state-open-data][^digital-science-contracting] Digital Science's government-contracting page states that it collaborates with U.S. federal agencies and lists Figshare among the research solutions offered for government research, discovery, planning, and reporting.[^digital-science-contracting] Figshare also publishes guidance directed at U.S. federally funded researchers.[^figshare-us-funder]

The conflict-of-interest question is not whether Digital Science or Figshare received a specific NASA instruction regarding Lehti's work. That has not been established. The narrower question is whether a repository provider with government-agency relationships should provide transparent, itemized moderation reasons when removing academic criticism of a major U.S. government agency.

## DOI and tombstone concerns

The DOI issue is separate from the removal issue. A platform may remove files, but DOI-linked scholarly records still require durable identification if the work existed and was cited. Zenodo's policy says withdrawn records should have tombstone pages, reasons for withdrawal, and retained DOI and URL records.[^zenodo-policies] Figshare states that metadata are accessible even when data are no longer available and that metadata are retained for the lifetime of the repository.[^figshare-fair]

For this reason, a generic removal page or broken page is insufficient for scholarly traceability. A proper tombstone should show, at minimum:

* title;
* author or creator;
* DOI;
* publication date;
* item type;
* version history if applicable;
* removal or access-disabled status;
* removal date;
* specific removal reason;
* replacement or archive link if available;
* appeal or contact path.

Without those fields, a removed DOI no longer functions as a stable scholarly record.

## Why this matters

Academic repositories are citation infrastructure. They mediate public access to research, preserve metadata, and shape what future readers can verify. When removals are opaque, three forms of damage occur:

1. **Author damage:** the author loses access to works, metadata, DOI records, and publication history.
2. **Reader damage:** readers cannot inspect the removed work or evaluate whether removal was justified.
3. **Record damage:** DOI-linked scholarship becomes incomplete, misleading, or invisible.

The danger is not only that one author loses files. The larger danger is that controversial academic material can be removed under broad administrative labels while the platform continues to present itself as open, persistent, and research-supportive.

## Counterarguments and limitations

Several counterarguments must be preserved:

* Figshare and Zenodo may have enforced neutral policies unrelated to viewpoint.
* Some removed works may have violated repository suitability rules even if the author disputes that conclusion.
* Repetitive uploads, duplicate material, formatting, metadata errors, or platform-specific rules could have contributed to enforcement.
* A platform's relationship with NASA, CERN, U.S. agencies, or Digital Science does not prove any removal was directed by those institutions.
* Search absence does not prove suppression unless the search method is reproducible and comparable across platforms.
* Correspondence, DOI traces, and platform notices document what was communicated, but they do not reveal internal moderation reasoning.
* Some search reports conflict because they used different inclusion rules: DOI-only versus non-DOI, paper-only versus datasets, direct denial versus broader skepticism, and public landing-page metadata versus secondary traces.

These limitations do not erase the accountability question. They define the evidence still needed.

## Open questions

1. Which exact Figshare items were removed, disabled, hidden, or detached from DOI landing pages?
2. What policy clause was applied to each affected Figshare item?
3. Did any complaint, copyright claim, defamation concern, research-integrity review, automated detection, or external notification trigger the Figshare action?
4. Did Figshare preserve the files, metadata, thumbnails, hashes, DOI state history, and audit logs?
5. Can each affected Figshare DOI resolve to a specific tombstone with title, creator, DOI, and removal reason?
6. Why did Zenodo classify at least one Lehti-associated record as spam?
7. Did Zenodo apply a spam label to all of Lehti's records or only to specific DOIs?
8. Why did Zenodo provide both a spam classification and a broad Terms of Use clause?
9. Were any Moon-landing-critical works by other authors present on Zenodo or Figshare before the April 2026 N-K Sciences records?
10. Did platform relationships with NASA, CERN, Digital Science, or U.S. government agencies create review bias, reputational risk sensitivity, or institutional reluctance to host NASA-critical material?
11. What appeal process exists for authors whose DOI-linked academic records are removed?
12. What public standard should open repositories follow when removing controversial academic criticism?

## Investigative conclusion

The evidence supports a serious repository-transparency dispute.

ResearchGate and Academia.edu provide a useful comparison. They contained accessible Apollo-critical or Apollo-denial material, including technical-style material, rhetorical material, and a retracted item. That record is not an endorsement of those claims. It is evidence that controversial material can remain visible, searchable, and contestable.

Zenodo and Figshare show a different pattern in the supplied record. Figshare hosted DOI-linked Apollo-critical material by Andrew Lehti, after which multiple records were reported removed or unavailable under broad terms language. Zenodo did not yield a visible historical corpus of Apollo-critical DOI literature in extended searches; the accessible Apollo-critical Zenodo cluster found in the later report appears to date from April 6, 2026, while older Lehti-associated Zenodo records were tombstoned as spam.

The strongest defensible conclusion is that the removals and absence pattern require itemized disclosure, DOI-level preservation, and independent audit. The stronger claim that NASA, CERN, Digital Science, Figshare, or Zenodo coordinated viewpoint-based censorship remains a hypothesis. It is supported by timing, subject matter, search asymmetry, and conflict-of-interest concerns, but it requires internal records, reproduced searches, or direct admissions to become established fact.

The scientific standard remains simple: if a claim is wrong, preserve it and refute it. If a record violates policy, preserve the metadata and state the violation. Open science cannot depend on trust in unseen moderation.
